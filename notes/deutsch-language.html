Reverso Context — translations in real sentences

context.reverso.net is one of the best sites for learning German. Instead of a single dictionary meaning, it shows every common way a phrase is translated, with real example sentences from books, films and websites.

Example: searching "ich darf" (German → English) gives I can, I would, I may, I'm, I should, let me, may I, I get to, I could, I'll, I might, allow me, I am allowed, mind if I, you don't mind. This shows how one short German phrase can have many English meanings depending on the context.

Die Wochentage (Days of the Week)

die Woche = the week (plural die Wochen), der Tag = the day (plural die Tage), die Wochentage = the days of the week. Every day of the week is masculine, so it always takes der. Notes from November 22, 2026.

Tip: it works the same way in Bangla — সোম is the moon (Montag) and রবি is the sun (Sonntag).

Today, tomorrow, yesterday

GermanEnglish
Heute ist Sonntag.Today is Sunday.
Morgen ist Montag.Tomorrow is Monday.
Gestern war Samstag.Yesterday was Saturday.

war is the past form of ist (was / is). To say "on Monday", use am: Am Montag lerne ich Deutsch. (On Monday I learn German.)

Articles & Plural (der / die / das)

Every German noun has an article that shows its gender, and nouns are always written with a capital letter. In the plural the article is always die, whatever the gender.

GenderSingularPluralEnglish
Masculineder Stiftdie Stiftepen → pens
Femininedie Flaschedie Flaschenbottle → bottles
Neuterdas Handydie Handysmobile phone → mobile phones
Masculineder Tagdie Tageday → days
Femininedie Wochedie Wochenweek → weeks

Tip: the plural article is always die, but the plural ending changes (-e, -n, -s, …) and there's no single rule. Learn every new noun as a set: article + word + plural, e.g. der Stift, die Stifte.

Verb Conjugation (Subject–Verb Agreement)

Just like subject–verb agreement in English (I go / he goes), a German verb changes its ending depending on the subject — but German changes it for every person, not just he/she/it.

How to conjugate a regular verb in the present tense:

  1. Take the infinitive, e.g. gehen (to go).
  2. Cancel (remove) the -en ending → the stem is geh.
  3. Add the ending that matches the subject: e, st, t, en, t, en.

Formula: Stem + e, st, t, en, t, en

SubjectEndinggehen (to go)English
Ich-egeheI go
Du-stgehstYou go (informal)
Er / sie / es-tgehtHe / she / it goes
Wir-engehenWe go
Ihr-tgehtYou go (plural, informal)
sie / Sie-engehenThey go / You go (formal)

Tip: the wir and sie/Sie forms always look the same as the infinitive. When the stem ends in -t or -d (arbeiten, finden), add an extra e so the verb is easier to say: du arbeitest, er arbeitet, ihr findet.

W-Questions (W-Fragen)

German question words almost all start with W (like English "wh-" words). In a W-question the question word comes first and the conjugated verb comes second: Wo wohnst du? (Where do you live?)

Modal Verbs

Modal verbs (can, must, should, may, like, want, would like) change with the subject, but they are irregular: they don't follow the normal stem + e, st, t, en, t, en rule. Note that the ich and er/sie/es forms are always the same and have no ending (ich kann, er kann).

Word order with modal verbs

  • Put the modal verb right after the subject in statements (position 2).
  • Put the main verb (infinitive) at the very end of the sentence.
  • In yes/no questions, the modal verb comes first: Können wir … ?

Ich kann sehr gut Deutsch sprechen. — I can speak German very well.

GermanEnglish

Ich will jetzt gehen.I want to go now.
Ich möchte etwas zu trinken.I would like something to drink.
Können wir uns morgen treffen?Can we meet tomorrow?
Willst du mit mir fernsehen?Do you want to watch TV with me?

Darf ich dich sehen?May I see you?
Darf ich deinen Stift nehmen?May I take your pen?

Note: sollten (in "Wir sollten das nicht machen") is the "should" form of sollen — softer advice than sollen.

Everyday Phrases

Short phrases for daily life: saying thanks and sorry, reacting, asking for things, and getting by when I don't understand. My handwritten notes from November 25, 2026.

Thank you, please & sorry

DeutschEnglish
Dankeschön!Thank you (very much)!
Bitteschön! / Bitte!You're welcome!
Es tut mir leid.I'm sorry.
Es tut mir so leid.I'm so sorry.
Entschuldigung!Sorry! / Excuse me!

Tip: Bitte has several meanings: "please", "you're welcome", and (in Wie bitte?) "pardon?". Use Entschuldigung to get someone's attention or for small mistakes; Es tut mir leid is for real regret.

Reacting in a conversation

DeutschEnglish
Alles ok. / Alles gut.Everything is OK. / All good.
Kein Problem.No problem.
Keine Sorge.Don't worry.
Egal.It doesn't matter.
Genau!Exactly! / That's right!
Doch!Yes, it is! / Yes, I do! (correcting a "no")
Keine Ahnung.No idea.
Was ist passiert?What happened?

Tip: Doch means "yes" when you disagree with a negative. Du sprichst kein Deutsch, oder? — Doch! (You don't speak German, right? — Yes, I do!) In Bangla it feels like আছে তো! / হ্যাঁ তো!

Where is …? (Wo ist …?)

DeutschEnglish
Wo ist der Bahnhof?Where is the train station?
Wo ist mein Handy?Where is my phone?
Wo ist mein Stift?Where is my pen?
Wo kann ich Salz finden?Where can I find salt?

Careful: das Salz = salt, der Salat = salad. They sound close but are different words.

When I don't understand

DeutschEnglish
Wie bitte?Pardon? (I didn't catch what you said.)
Was?What? (casual)
Ich spreche ein bisschen Deutsch.I speak a little German.
Sprechen Sie Englisch?Do you speak English?
Können Sie es bitte wiederholen?Can you please repeat it?
Können Sie bitte ein bisschen langsamer sprechen?Can you please speak a little more slowly?

Note: Wie bitte? is more polite than Was? Use langsamer ("slower"), not langsam ("slow"), when asking someone to slow down. These questions use Sie, the polite "you", for strangers.
